### Why Do We Need This?

As far as I can tell, every other crate that does unit systems defines some amount of the the types you will use, or has macros do it, or relies on direct method calls instead of traits, this means:
- You can't choose the memory layout, or naming. A breaking library change is a breaking change for you too.
- The crate probably only supports limited number types, or has tons of flags for enabling each specific third party type.
- You often can't `#[derive()]` or `impl` your own traits (orphan rule!), so you end up with tons of flags for enabling each third party trait.
- You may have to learn a special DSL, which may not support controlling `pub` or or adding extra fields (none that I've seen) or doc comments.
- If it has a macro to generate everything at once, chances are that the things it generates are hardcoded to work with eachother without traits.
- It's unclear what types get generated, where they are, what they're named.
- You can't create your own special sauce implementation without common ground traits.
- It may be hard to extend as you please - E.G. integrating dynamic units/ unit types in a UI. The traits may have prohibitively complex generics.

Crates like `serde` and `clap` let you define your own types and customize in depth how the derive macros generate standard traits, and I wanted this for dimensional analysis too. Reflection solves some, but not all these problems.

### The Idea

yadc gives you **patterns, a very small set of traits, and derive macros** so you can build *your own* unit system - one where code reads like dimensional algebra on a whiteboard:

- **You own every type.** The dimension struct, the kind enum, the quantity type, the dynamic quantity, every unit and every constant: all declared in your crate, with your names, your `pub`, your doc comments, your extra derives. yadc has no wrapper types and no structs you build your units from.
- **Modelling choices are yours.** Is angle a dimension axis or a label on a plain number? Is a temperature a point, or an amount kept apart from its differences? Which sums have no result? That is data in *your* system, not a decision baked into the library. [`yadc-si`](yadc-si/) is just one such system, generated from [`yadc-codegen/si.toml`](yadc-codegen/si.toml).
- **Standard traits wherever they fit.** Quantities talk through `Mul`, `Div`, `Add`, `Sub`, `From`, `TryFrom`, `Display` and `num_traits::Inv`; yadc defines a trait only for what std cannot say: `Dimension` (std `Mul` / `Div` whose result may be a dimension or an `Option` of one, `Add` / `Sub` returning `Option`, a power and a root that may not exist, `DIMENSIONLESS` - dimension one - and `plain`, the label forgotten), `Quantity` (a value you can add, scale, and measure with), `Unit<Q, N>` (two functions between numbers and a `Q`), and one named one-way map, `Cast` (another number type).
- **Units are constants.** A unit belongs to no quantity: it is a value that implements `Unit<Q>` for whatever it measures. Every quantity constant is one (`KILOPASCAL(p)`); a scale is two of them, a zero and a step, in a tuple struct of yours (°C is `Scale(ICE_POINT, KELVIN)`, feet above sea level `Scale(SEA_LEVEL, FOOT)`), and a level is a scale on a logarithmic line (dBm is `Scale(MILLIWATT_LEVEL, DECIBEL)`); anything else is a type of yours with two hand-written functions (the Beaufort scale). An enum of units - for a menu, a parser - is `#[derive(Unit)]`, each variant naming the constant it is. Units never take part in arithmetic: you turn a number into a quantity with a unit, do the maths on quantities, and read the result with a unit.
- **Nothing hidden.** The traits are the whole contract: each derive writes its own trait, its supertraits, and the extras it lists (all on by default, an explicit `impl(..)` picks which) - trait impls only, no inherent methods or constants on your types - and anything a derive produces you can write by hand. yadc is the statics. What isn't about quantities lives in crates of its own, which yadc does not depend on: [`dynamickit`](dynamickit/) for dynamic types and closed sets, and [`yadc-math`](yadc-math/) for the generic maths (powers and roots that may not exist, fraction exponents).
- **Numbers only through units** (see [The Whiteboard Principle](#the-whiteboard-principle)). A quantity's number is never read or written directly: a number becomes a quantity, and a quantity a number, only through a unit, so every number in a program says which unit it is in. The base constants (`pub const METRE: Length = Qty(1.0);`, one per base unit, written where the quantity's field is visible) are the only raw numbers; everything else is defined from them the way a standards document writes it (`pub const PSI: Pressure = POUND * STANDARD_GRAVITY / (INCH * INCH);`).

### The Whiteboard Principle

**Every trait is a structure you could write on a whiteboard, and its bounds are exactly the operations that structure needs.** If a candidate trait or bound can't be named as a piece of mathematics, it doesn't go in; if the mathematics needs an operation, the trait asks for exactly that one and nothing more. That is what keeps the traits small, and it is how each design question here was settled.

| Trait | On the whiteboard (plain words, then the formal name) | It asks for |
|---|---|---|
| `Dimension` | exponents that add when you multiply, plus optionally a kind - an amount, a point, a logarithm, or a label of yours: a product and a quotient (always there among amounts; none where a point or a level is involved), a sum that may not exist and keeps its left operand | std `*` `/` (`Output: Into<Option<Self>>`), `+` `-` returning `Option`, `PartialPowi` and `PartialRoot`, `DIMENSIONLESS` - dimension one, the empty product (what `x / x` gives); `plain` (the label forgotten) is provided |
| `Quantity` | a value you can add and scale, which measures the others of its kind (a one-dimensional vector space that is its own chart) | `Copy`, `PartialOrd`, `+`, scaling by its numbers, and being a `Unit` of itself |
| `Unit<Q, N>` | two inverse functions between numbers and `Q` (an invertible map, a coordinate chart) | `quantity`, `value` |
| `PartialPowi` / `PartialRoot` | an n-th power / n-th root that may not exist; for a whole-number exponent, a root is exact division | `partial_powi` / `partial_root` |
| `Cast<T>` | a change of number type | `cast_into` (called as `x.cast::<f32>()`) |

What follows from it:

- **Numbers only through units.** A quantity constant reads a quantity as a ratio (`KILOPASCAL(p)` is `p / KILOPASCAL`, a plain number) and builds one by scaling (`KILOPASCAL(2.0)`); a scale adds a zero (`CELSIUS(n)` is `ICE_POINT + n · KELVIN`). No raw "get the number" method exists, because the whiteboard has none: you write the unit.
- **Axes and kinds.** A dimension is exponent *axes* - metres, seconds, kelvin, and in `yadc-si` radians - plus a *kind*, which says how the quantity behaves. An ordinary amount composes: every two multiply and divide, so every power and reciprocal exists and only a root can fail (there is no "metre to the ½" with whole-number exponents). A *point* (the affine kind) is measured from a zero somebody chose; a *logarithm* (the log kind) is a level or a gain in decibels; any other kind is a label of your own, which only matters to `+` and `-` (a torque is not an energy; an absolute temperature is not a temperature step). A sum keeps its left operand's type, as on the whiteboard. Beside a plain number a label survives (`2.0 * t`, an average `(t1 + t2) / 2.0`); multiplied by anything with units it drops (`R * t` is just J/mol). Nothing computes a kind: a constant that has it gives it, `n * BEL`.
- **Partial operations return `Option`.** "May not exist" is written in the type, with std operators, and partial steps compose with `?`: on a value whose dimension is known only at run time, `((a * b)? + c)?` - a product of a point has no result, nor has a sum of a length and a time.
- **A point is not a quantity.** A quantity's zero is a fact (no mass, no length), so it scales, multiplies and adds. An altitude's zero is a convention - sea level, or this runway - so `2 × altitude` would be a different place depending on which zero you picked. A point's kind is affine: two points of one dimension differ by an amount, a point moves by one, and nothing else compiles. A point is not a `Quantity`; it is read on a scale, a zero and a step (feet above sea level, or above *this* runway), and every scale of one dimension reads the same line.
- **Differences are what make a number line.** A scale whose values have no differences - a ranking, a hardness number - has no steps to count, so it can't be scaled; it's a label that does not add to itself.
- **Printing is std `Display`.** A unit prints its symbol (`{}`) or name (`{:#}`); a quantity in a unit is the number and the unit: `format!("{:.1} {}", unit.value(q), unit)`.
- **Std traits wherever std already names the structure:** the operators, `From` / `TryFrom` (one shape inside another, and the partial way back), `Display` / `FromStr`.

### Quick Start

Using the ready-made SI system, a crate needs only `#![feature(generic_const_exprs)]`:

```rust
#![feature(generic_const_exprs)]
#![allow(incomplete_features)]

use yadc::Unit;
use yadc_si::DynamicSIQuantity;
use yadc_si::mechanical::consts::{
    DBM, DBW, DECIBEL, HOUR, KILOGRAM, KILOMETRE, KILOMETRE_PER_HOUR, METRE_PER_SECOND_SQUARED,
    MILE, POUND,
};
use yadc_si::mechanical::*;
use yadc_si::thermal::consts::{CELSIUS, FAHRENHEIT};
use yadc_si::thermal::{TemperatureInterval, ThermodynamicTemperature};

// A unit turns a number into a quantity, and a quantity back into a number. There
// is no other way in or out - and every constant is a unit.
let m = POUND(10.0);                           // a Mass, stored as 4.5359237 kg
let kg = KILOGRAM(m);                             // 4.5359237
println!("{:.1} {}", MassUnit::Kilogram.value(m), MassUnit::Kilogram); // "4.5 kg" ({:#}: "kilograms")

// Maths is on quantities, and the types follow the dimensions.
let v: Velocity = KILOMETRE(90.0) / HOUR(1.0);
let kmh = KILOMETRE_PER_HOUR(v);                  // 90.0
let f: Force = m * METRE_PER_SECOND_SQUARED(9.81);
// let nope = m + v;                                    // error: cannot add: the dimensions differ

// °C is a scale, `Scale(ICE_POINT, KELVIN)`: a zero plus steps of 1 K - the unit's business, not yours.
let t = CELSIUS(25.0);                                  // a ThermodynamicTemperature: 298.15 K
let fahrenheit = FAHRENHEIT(t);                         // 77.0
let room = CELSIUS(20.0);
let rise: TemperatureInterval = t - room;               // T - T = ΔT (5 K)
// let nope = rise + t;                                 // error: cannot add: the labels have no sum (write t + rise)
let twice: ThermodynamicTemperature = 2.0 * t;          // a temperature is an amount: 596.3 K

// dBm is a scale too, on the logarithmic line: `Scale(MILLIWATT_LEVEL, DECIBEL)`. It reads a
// `PowerLevel` - where gains add; the watts are one std `From` away.
let rx: PowerLevel = DBM(30.0);
let p = Power::from(rx);                                // 1 W
let dbw = DBW(PowerLevel::from(p));                     // 0.0
let out = rx - 3.0 * DECIBEL + 13.0 * DECIBEL;          // a link budget: 40 dBm
// let nope = out + out;                                // error: cannot add: the labels have no sum (it would be the logarithm of W²)

// When the dimension is only known at run time: the same operators, each returning an
// Option - a sum of a speed and a time has no result, nor has a product of a level.
let d = (DynamicSIQuantity::from(KILOMETRE(90.0)) / DynamicSIQuantity::from(HOUR(2.0))).unwrap();
let v: Velocity = d.try_into().unwrap();                // Err(d) on a mismatch
let kmh = KILOMETRE_PER_HOUR(v);                  // 45.0
assert_eq!(d + DynamicSIQuantity::from(HOUR(1.0)), None); // nonsense has no result
// so a run-time calculation reads ((a * b)? + c)?

// A unit picked at run time: some unit of length, checked at compile time.
let metric = true;
let u: &dyn Unit<Length> = if metric { &KILOMETRE } else { &MILE };
println!("{:.1}", u.value(u.quantity(12.0)));           // 12.0, in whichever unit was picked
```

### Units

A unit is a value: a named way to turn a number into a quantity and back. It has no dimension and no quantity of its own: `impl Unit<Q> for U` *is* the declaration "U measures Q", and its two methods - `quantity` and `value` - *are* the conversion. A unit is a constant, of types that are yours; three shapes cover everything, and an enum names any mix of them:

| Shape | In plain words | Build | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| a quantity constant | a kilometre is `1000.0 * METRE` | `KILOMETRE * n` | `q / KILOMETRE` |
| a scale, `Scale(zero, step)` | °C starts at `ICE_POINT` and counts kelvins; dBm starts at one milliwatt and counts decibels | `zero + step.quantity(n)` | `step.value(q - zero)` |
| a type with a hand-written `impl Unit` | the Beaufort wind scale is a formula, not a scale factor | your function | your function |

```rust
pub const WATT: Power = JOULE / SECOND;
pub const ICE_POINT: Temperature = ABSOLUTE_ZERO + 273.15 * KELVIN;

pub const CELSIUS: Scale<Temperature, TemperatureStep> = Scale(ICE_POINT, KELVIN);     // a scale: a zero and a step
pub const FEET_AMSL: Scale<Altitude, Length> = Scale(SEA_LEVEL, FOOT);                 // a scale of points
let agl = Scale(terrain, FOOT);                                                   // a zero known at run time

#[derive(Copy, Clone, yadc::Unit)]                     // a choice of units, for a menu
#[unit(of = Temperature)]
pub enum TemperatureUnit {
    #[unit("K", "kelvin", by = Scale(ABSOLUTE_ZERO, KELVIN))]
    Kelvin,
    #[unit("°C", "degrees Celsius", by = CELSIUS)]
    Celsius,
}
```

**Levels: decibels.** A decibel counts on a logarithmic line, where multiplying is adding: gains of 3 dB and 10 dB make 13 dB, where the ratios 2 and 10 make 20. That line is two more quantities of yours, stored as plain numbers like any other: a `Gain` (a step on the line, in decibels - gains add) and a `PowerLevel` (a place on it: two levels differ by a gain, a gain moves a level, two levels have no sum). The logarithm itself appears once, in the std `From` between the line and the watts - and a scale on the line measures both:

```rust
pub const BEL: Gain = Qty(1.0);                        // base constants of the line
pub const WATT_LEVEL: PowerLevel = Qty(0.0);
pub const DECIBEL: Gain = 0.1 * BEL;
pub const MILLIWATT_LEVEL: PowerLevel = WATT_LEVEL - 30.0 * DECIBEL;
pub const DBM: Scale<PowerLevel, Gain> = Scale(MILLIWATT_LEVEL, DECIBEL);   // dBm: decibels from one milliwatt

impl From<PowerLevel> for Power { fn from(l: PowerLevel) -> Power { WATT * 10f64.powf(BEL(l - WATT_LEVEL)) } }
impl From<Power> for PowerLevel { fn from(p: Power) -> PowerLevel { WATT_LEVEL + BEL(WATT(p).log10()) } }

let p = Power::from(DBM(30.0));                  // 1 W: to the linear side
let n = DBM(PowerLevel::from(2.0 * p));          // 33.01
```

A level and a gain are both the log kind, told apart by their exponents: a `PowerLevel` carries the exponents of power, and a `Gain` none - so a level less a level is a gain because watts over watts is a plain ratio, and two levels sum to the logarithm of watts squared, which no scale of power reads.

A few consequences worth spelling out:

- **Units never take part in arithmetic.** `KILOMETRE(90.0) / HOUR(1.0)` is a velocity; read it with any unit of velocity. (A quantity constant *is* a quantity, so `KILOMETRE / HOUR` is a velocity constant too - and a unit of velocity.)
- **A step, a zero and a scale are different things with different names.** `KELVIN` and `DEGREE_FAHRENHEIT` are steps (temperature differences); `ABSOLUTE_ZERO`, `ICE_POINT` and `FAHRENHEIT_ZERO` are zeros (temperatures); `CELSIUS` and `FAHRENHEIT` are scales (each a zero and a step, reading absolute temperatures).
- **A scale is a type of yours.** `pub struct Scale<P, S>(pub P, pub S);` with one hand-written `impl Unit` - `n` is `zero + n · step` - is all there is to it; the quantity knows nothing of scales. It is a unit of its zero's type alone: `DBM` measures a `PowerLevel`, and a `Power` is `Power::from(level)`.
- **How much you know is how much you erase.** `KILOMETRE` or `LengthUnit::Kilometre` is the exact unit; `&dyn Unit<Length>` is some unit of length, still checked at compile time; your enum of unit enums is some unit of some quantity (see "Sets" below).
- **Units print and parse with std traits.** The derive writes `Display` - `{}` is the symbol, `{:#}` the long name (`format!("{}", LengthUnit::Kilometre)` is `"km"`, `{:#}` is `"kilometres"`) - and `FromStr`, which reads the symbol back: `"km".parse::<LengthUnit>()`, or `Err(yadc::UnknownUnit)`. `#[unit(impl(..))]` lists which of the two to write (`impl()` for neither).
- **By hand, anything goes** - including a step known only at run time. Implement `Unit<Q>` on a struct with fields - `SensorTicks { per_tick: Length }` (see `examples/custom_unit_impl.rs`). A unit's numbers need not be one `f64`: a grid reads a position as `[f64; 3]`, a `Unit<Position, [f64; 3]>`. Another crate may write `impl Unit<its::Len> for yadc_si::mechanical::LengthUnit` for its own quantity: the orphan rule allows it.
- **A formula that is not a scale** - a road grade read as an angle, the Beaufort scale - is a hand-written `impl Unit`, or a `From` / `TryFrom` between two quantities of yours.

### Points: the affine kind

An altitude, an instant, the voltage at a node: each is measured from a zero someone chose (sea level, an epoch, ground). Two of them differ by a quantity, and one moves by a quantity - but doubling one, or adding two, gives an answer that depends on where the zero was put, so those don't compile. A point is a dimension whose kind is the one your dimension names as affine:

```rust
#[derive_const(PartialEq, Default)]
#[derive(Copy, Clone, Debug, Eq, ConstParamTy, yadc::Dimension)]
pub enum Kind { #[default] Linear, #[kind(affine)] Affine, #[kind(log)] Log }

#[derive_const(PartialEq)]
#[derive(Copy, Clone, Debug, Eq, ConstParamTy, yadc::Dimension)]
pub struct Dim { pub m: i8, pub s: i8, #[kind] pub kind: Kind }

pub type Altitude = Qty<{ Dim { m: 1, kind: Kind::Affine, ..Dim::DIMENSIONLESS } }>;
pub const SEA_LEVEL: Altitude = Qty(0.0);                                  // a base constant

pub const FEET_AMSL: Scale<Altitude, Length> = Scale(SEA_LEVEL, FOOT);              // a scale: a zero and a step

let aircraft = FEET_AMSL(6500.0);
let clearance: Length = aircraft - terrain;              // a difference of points is an amount
let halfway = a + (b - a) / 2.0;                         // a midpoint, without ever scaling a point
let agl = Scale(terrain, FOOT);                               // feet above *this* ground: a unit made at run time
// let nope = 2.0 * aircraft;                            // error: a point cannot be scaled
// let nope = aircraft + aircraft;                       // error: cannot add a point to a point
```

A point is not a `Quantity`. Which zero it was read from is not in its type: `Scale(SEA_LEVEL, FOOT)` and `Scale(runway, FOOT)` are two scales on the one line of altitudes, and GPS seconds and Unix seconds two on the one line of instants. (A temperature has a true zero, so a system chooses: a point, as strict as an altitude, or - as in `yadc-si` and below - an amount with a label, which scales and multiplies.) A position in 3-D is your own vector of points, and a site grid reading it as three numbers is a `Unit<Position, [f64; 3]>` (see `yadc/examples/rotations.rs`).

### Sets: a quantity chosen at run time

When the *quantity* is the run-time choice - a logger channel measures a pressure or a temperature; a setting says "show this as `[Pressure ▾]` in `[kPa ▾]`" - list the choices as your own enums. yadc gives the statics; the dynamic types and the enum plumbing come from [`dynamickit`](dynamickit/), which knows nothing about quantities. `yadc-si`'s dynamic type, `DynamicSIQuantity`, is one: `#[dynamic(StaticSIQuantity<const D: ISQ> => { dim: D, value: .0 })]` says converting (`From`) moves the number and records `D`, and the way back (`TryFrom`) checks it; `#[dynamic(impl(Mul -> Option<Self>, Add -> Option<Self>, ..))]` lifts each operator from the fields' own, its output stated.

```rust
use dynamickit::{Members, VariantVisitor, Variants, Via, Visit, Visitor};

/// What the app measures: a closed set.
#[derive(Clone, Copy, Debug, Members, Via)]
#[members(convert(DynamicSIQuantity))]        // From / TryFrom with the dynamic type, through the variants
#[via(DynamicSIQuantity, Add, Sub, Mul, Div, Mul<f64>, Div<f64>)] // the operators listed: computed there
enum Quantities { Pressure(Pressure), Temperature(ThermodynamicTemperature) }

/// The setting: which quantity, in which unit.
#[derive(Clone, Copy, PartialEq, Debug, Visit, Variants)]
enum Units { Pressure(PressureUnit), Temperature(ThermodynamicTemperatureUnit) }

/// Which quantity a unit family measures: a fact about the types, written as one.
trait Measures: Unit<Self::Quantity> { type Quantity; }
impl Measures for PressureUnit { type Quantity = Pressure; }
impl Measures for ThermodynamicTemperatureUnit { type Quantity = ThermodynamicTemperature; }

/// Build and read, once for every unit family: two visitors, over std `From` / `TryFrom`.
struct Build(f64);
impl<U: Measures> Visitor<U> for Build where Quantities: From<U::Quantity> {
    type Output = Quantities;
    fn visit(self, u: U) -> Quantities { Quantities::from(u.quantity(self.0)) }
}
struct Read(Quantities);
impl<U: Measures> Visitor<U> for Read where U::Quantity: TryFrom<Quantities> {
    type Output = Option<f64>;
    fn visit(self, u: U) -> Option<f64> { Some(u.value(U::Quantity::try_from(self.0).ok()?)) }
}

let kpa = Units::Pressure(PressureUnit::Kilopascal);
let q: Quantities = kpa.visit_with(Build(101.325));    // a sensor number, in kPa
let n = Units::Pressure(PressureUnit::Bar).visit_with(Read(q)); // Some(1.01325); None for another quantity
let sum = q + q;                                       // Some(pressure); None where the open type has no result
let doubled = q * 2.0;                                 // scaling keeps the variant
Units::each_variant(&mut menu);                        // the menus: each group's name and constructor,
                                                       // handed to a `VariantVisitor` of yours
```

The derives write trait impls only - of std traits, and dynamickit's `Visit` and `Variants`. Your traits on the set are yours to write, a few lines each through a `Visit` visitor. See [`yadc-si/examples/`](yadc-si/examples/).

### Defining Your Own System

Everything is a type you declare; the derives connect them. A minimal system with lengths and temperatures (a complete one, with points and levels, is [`yadc-tests/src/lib.rs`](yadc-tests/src/lib.rs)):

```rust
#![feature(generic_const_exprs, min_adt_const_params, const_trait_impl)]
#![feature(const_cmp, const_default, derive_const, const_ops, const_convert, const_try)]
#![allow(incomplete_features)]

use core::marker::ConstParamTy;
use yadc::Dimension;

// The kinds: how a quantity behaves. `#[default]` marks the ordinary one, an
// amount. `Absolute` is a label of this system's own, for a temperature: an
// amount too, but two of them differ by a linear step, and a step moves one.
// A sum with no result is `None`: there is no "invalid" label.
#[derive_const(PartialEq, Default)]
#[derive(Copy, Clone, Debug, Eq, ConstParamTy, yadc::Dimension)]
pub enum Kind {
    #[default]
    Linear,
    #[kind(sub(Absolute) = Linear, add(Linear) = Absolute, sub(Linear) = Absolute)]
    Absolute,
}

// One exponent per base axis, plus the kind. (The `#[kind]` field is
// optional; it may also name an affine kind, for points, and a logarithmic
// one, for levels.) The derive writes the const std operators, the power and
// the root, and `Display` (`m·s⁻¹`).
#[derive_const(PartialEq)]
#[derive(Copy, Clone, Debug, Eq, ConstParamTy, yadc::Dimension)]
pub struct Dim { pub m: i8, pub s: i8, pub k: i8, #[kind] pub kind: Kind }

// The static quantity: the dimension is a const parameter. The derive writes
// the operators and `Quantity` (so every constant is a unit of its
// own) and the extras (`Display`, `Neg`, `+=`, powers, ...) - nothing that
// reads or writes the number. The field is private: only this crate writes
// it, in the base constants.
#[derive(Copy, Clone, PartialEq, PartialOrd, Debug, yadc::Quantity)]
pub struct Qty<const D: Dim>(f64);

pub type Length = Qty<{ Dim { m: 1, ..Dim::DIMENSIONLESS } }>;
pub type Time = Qty<{ Dim { s: 1, ..Dim::DIMENSIONLESS } }>;
pub type Temperature = Qty<{ Dim { k: 1, kind: Kind::Absolute, ..Dim::DIMENSIONLESS } }>;
pub type TemperatureStep = Qty<{ Dim { k: 1, ..Dim::DIMENSIONLESS } }>;

// The base constants: the only raw numbers. A temperature has two - a step
// (a kelvin is a `TemperatureStep`) and absolute zero.
pub const METRE: Length = Qty(1.0);
pub const SECOND: Time = Qty(1.0);
pub const KELVIN: TemperatureStep = Qty(1.0);
pub const ABSOLUTE_ZERO: Temperature = Qty(0.0);

// Everything else, from them, with the quantities' own `const` operators -
// and each is a unit.
pub const KILOMETRE: Length = 1000.0 * METRE;
pub const ICE_POINT: Temperature = ABSOLUTE_ZERO + 273.15 * KELVIN;

// A scale is a constant too: a zero and a step, in a type of yours that is a unit.
#[derive(Copy, Clone)]
pub struct Scale<P, S>(pub P, pub S);
const impl<P, S, N> yadc::Unit<P, N> for Scale<P, S>
where
    P: Copy + [const] Sub + [const] Add<<P as Sub>::Output, Output = P>,
    S: [const] yadc::Unit<<P as Sub>::Output, N>,
{
    fn quantity(&self, value: N) -> P { self.0 + self.1.quantity(value) }
    fn value(&self, quantity: P) -> N { self.1.value(quantity - self.0) }
}
pub const CELSIUS: Scale<Temperature, TemperatureStep> = Scale(ICE_POINT, KELVIN);

// A family of named units, picked at run time: each variant says what it is.
#[derive(Copy, Clone, yadc::Unit)]
#[unit(of = Length)]
pub enum LengthUnit {
    #[unit("m", "metres", by = METRE)]
    Metre,
    #[unit("km", "kilometres", by = KILOMETRE)]
    Kilometre,
}

#[derive(Copy, Clone, yadc::Unit)]
#[unit(of = Temperature)]
pub enum TemperatureUnit {
    #[unit("K", "kelvin", by = Scale(ABSOLUTE_ZERO, KELVIN))]
    Kelvin,
    #[unit("°C", "degrees Celsius", by = CELSIUS)]
    Celsius,
}
```

Add any derives you like next to these (`serde`, `JsonSchema`, `Reflect`, ...); the orphan rule never gets in the way, because the types are yours. If you write a `Display` of your own for a quantity or a dimension, leave the derived one out: `#[quantity(impl(..))]` lists the extras to write, `#[dimension(impl())]` writes none. A type holding a quantity of any dimension at run time is yours as well, made with [`dynamickit`](dynamickit/)'s `#[derive(Dynamic)]` - `yadc-si`'s `DynamicSIQuantity` is one, generated by `yadc-codegen`.

**A temperature and a difference, without a wrapper type.** The `Absolute` label tells a temperature (measured from absolute zero) from a temperature difference: temperature − temperature = difference, temperature ± difference = temperature, and `difference + temperature` does not compile. That is just the label's three rules on your own `Kind`, so `t2 - t1` is a `TemperatureStep` and `t + 5.0 * KELVIN` a `Temperature`. Absolute zero is a fact, so a temperature still scales and multiplies - `n * R * t`, `(t1 + t2) / 2.0`. A system that would rather refuse those makes it a point instead: the affine kind, above.

**Constants are compile-time quantities.** The derives write the quantity operators, a constant's `Unit` and a derived unit enum's `Unit` as `const impl`s, and a scale's `Unit` is a `const impl` of yours, so constants compose in `const` items, evaluated by the compiler, and a dimension is checked there too:

```rust
pub const MARATHON: Length = 42.195 * KILOMETRE;
pub const BOILING: Temperature = CELSIUS(100.0);
pub const ABOVE_ROOM: TemperatureStep = BOILING - CELSIUS(20.0);
pub const BOILING_K: f64 = TemperatureUnit::Kelvin.value(BOILING); // 373.15, read at compile time
```

`yadc-si` generates them: its `base` module holds the base constants, and every family module a `consts` module defining the rest from them (`mechanical::consts::NEWTON` is `METRE * KILOGRAM / (SECOND * SECOND)`, `KILOMETRE` is `1000.0 * METRE`; `thermal::consts::ICE_POINT`). **Physical constants are units too** (`SPEED_OF_LIGHT`, `STANDARD_GRAVITY`).

**Other number types.** With the number type a parameter (`Qty<const D: Dim, N = f64>(N)`), `KILOPASCAL.cast::<f32>()` is the same pressure counted in `f32` (`const`), and std `From` widens where the number converts losslessly: `Pressure::from(p32)`.

**Reciprocals** follow from the algebra too: `num_traits::Inv` is written on every quantity whose inverse the dimension allows, so `SECOND(2.0).inv()` is a frequency.

**Powers and roots.** With `yadc::PowiAs` / `yadc::RootAs` in scope, `length.powi::<2>()` is an `Area` and `area.root::<2>()` a `Length` again; `time.powi::<-1>()` is a frequency. Powers are `const` (`const BOX: Volume = EDGE.powi::<3>();`). Every power of an amount exists; with whole-number exponents only exact roots do (`√m²` but not `√m`: "this root has no dimension").

**Fractional exponents.** Some units need halves: amplifier noise is quoted in volts per *square-root* hertz (V/√Hz), and the old CGS-Gaussian unit of charge is g^½·cm^(3/2)·s⁻¹. Make your dimension's exponent fields `yadc::Exponent` (a fraction: `Exponent::int(2)`, `Exponent::new(-5, 2)`) instead of `i8`, and every root exists - `noise / bandwidth.root::<2>()` is a V/√Hz. Nothing else changes; see `yadc/examples/fractional_exponents.rs`.

**The compiler says why.** A refused operation does not compile, and the message names the reason - "cannot add: the dimensions differ", "cannot add: the labels have no sum", "a point cannot be scaled", "cannot multiply: these have no product", "this root has no dimension" (the list, operator by operator, is in `yadc::gates`; the [`yadc-tests`](yadc-tests/) crate pins every one, in the crate that defines a system and in one that uses it). Two operators are limited by rustc itself: a compound assignment (`a += b`) reports std's "cannot add-assign ..." with the reason in a `help:` line, and unary minus on a point reports only "cannot apply unary operator `-`", with no reason.

### Column Explanations

Apologies if I've got some of these columns wrong.

- **Active** — updated within the last two years (checked May 2026). ✗ does not mean unusable - it's not like unit systems change much.
- **`no_std`** — can be used without the Rust standard library (e.g. on embedded targets).
- **Own types** — you define all the involves types in your own crate; the library only provides traits and derives. ✗ means the library defines the struct and you depend on it directly, or macros define it for you.
- **Dim Vector** — multiplying two quantities automatically produces the correct output type without you manually registering the relationship. E.g. `mass * accel` gives you a force-typed value purely from the dimension exponent arithmetic. ✗ means you must define each quantity and its operations by hand, or use explicit algebraic derivation macros.
- **Stores In Base Units** — the stored value is a raw number in canonical base units; the unit plays no role in storage. ✗ means either the unit is baked into the value's scale (so `5 km` stores `5.0`, not `5000.0`) or a unit tag is packed alongside the value at runtime.
- **Custom Value Type** — the underlying numeric type is customizable, so you can use `f32`, `i64`, `Vector3<f32>`, or any custom type. ✗ means the library is hardwired to one type. "fixed list" means the library supports only certain types it has added support for.
- **Angles/Absolute** — the type system distinguishes angle quantities from plain dimensionless ratios, and distinguishes absolute temperatures from temperature intervals, so `T − T = ΔT` and `ΔT + T` is a compile error. (yadc-si counts radians on an exponent axis and labels absolute temperatures `Absolute`.)
- **UCUM Serde** — whether the serde wire format uses [UCUM](https://ucum.org) unit strings. `dim` means the quantity's *dimension* is expressed as a UCUM base-unit composition (e.g. `m.kg.s-2`, `rad`, `K{Absolute}`), with the stored value always in base units — round-trip lossless for base unit symbols. `unit` means the specific *storage unit* is expressed as a UCUM string (value may not be in base units). `dimensioned`'s UCUM feature is a compile-time unit system of UCUM-named constants, not a serde format.
- **Full SI Coverage** — ships with a complete, ready-to-use SI implementation covering all ISQ quantities and units.
- **Static** — dimensions are checked at compile time; a wrong-dimension operation fails to compile.
- **Dynamic** — dimensions are checked at runtime; a mismatched-dim operation is reported as an `Option` / `Result` (in yadc-si: every operator of `DynamicSIQuantity` returns an `Option`, `None` where there is no result, chained with `?`).
- **Fully Trait Based** — the library exposes public traits (`Quantity`, `Unit`, …) as abstraction boundaries so you can write functions that are generic over *any* conforming quantity type — including quantities from unit systems the library has never seen. ✗ means the library provides only concrete types (even if parameterized); a function written against those types is always tied to one unit system.

### Nightly features required

Up front, this is a pretty experimental crate and relies on a bunch of fairly unstable nightly features, the goal is 'no compromise, with what nightly has'.

| What your crate does | Required at crate root |
|---|---|
| Just uses already-defined aliases and units (`Mass`, `POUND(10.0)`, …) | `generic_const_exprs` |
| … and does quantity arithmetic in `const` items (`const LIMIT: Velocity = 50.0 * KILOMETRE / HOUR;`) | additionally `const_trait_impl`, `const_ops` |
| Defines its own `Qty<const D: Dim>`, custom `Dim` / `Kind`, or new `pub type Foo = Qty<{ … }>;` aliases | `generic_const_exprs`, `min_adt_const_params`, `const_trait_impl`, `const_ops`, `const_cmp`, `const_default`, `const_convert`, `const_try`, `derive_const` |
| … whose units are called (`KILOMETRE(3.0)`: the `Fn` extra, on by default), or with a generic number type (`struct Qty<const D: Dim, N = f64>(N)`) | additionally `fn_traits`, `unboxed_closures`, `const_destruct` |
| Defines unit enums (`#[derive(Unit)]`) | `const_trait_impl` (and `fn_traits`, `unboxed_closures`, `const_destruct` for its `Fn` extra) |
| Uses `dynamickit`'s derives on your own types and sets | `const_trait_impl`, `const_convert` (plus `const_ops`, `const_cmp` and `const_destruct` for a dynamic type, `const_default` where a field is filled with `Default`, `const_destruct` for `Members`) |

Plus `#![allow(incomplete_features)]` to silence the `generic_const_exprs` warning.

- **`generic_const_exprs`** — needed wherever a value's *type* involves a const expression, e.g. `Qty<{ A * B }>` (the `Output` of `Mass * Acceleration`). Every consumer trips this the moment they write `mass * accel`, so every consumer needs the flag.
- **`min_adt_const_params`** — to write a const generic whose type is a user-defined ADT (`Qty<const D: Dim>(f64)` itself, or a new alias `pub type Foo = Qty<{ Dim { … } }>;`), and for the `#[derive(ConstParamTy)]` your `Dim` and `Kind` need.
- **`const_trait_impl`** — the derives write `const impl`s: of your dimension's and label's std operators, `PartialPowi` and `PartialRoot`, so the algebra can run inside const-generic bounds, and of the quantity operators, `Quantity` and `Unit`, so quantities and units work in `const` items.
- **`const_ops`**, **`const_convert`** — those `const impl`s are of core's operators and `From` / `TryFrom` / `Into`, which are not yet stable as const traits. Calling the operators in a `const` item needs `const_ops` too.
- **`const_cmp`**, **`const_default`** — calling core's const `PartialEq` and `Default` in const context (kind comparison, the zero exponent, the ordinary kind).
- **`const_try`** — the `Dimension` derive's const `+`, `-` and root use `?` on an `Option`, so a crate defining a `Dim` needs it.
- **`const_destruct`** — only when the number type is a generic parameter: a `const` operator must be able to drop a value it knows nothing about.
- **`derive_const`** — `#[derive_const(PartialEq)]` on your `Dim` and `Kind` (and `Default` on a `Kind`), so `==` on them is const-callable.

## Highlights

- **Const-generic dimensions.** `Qty<const D: Dim>` parameterizes the quantity type by a dimension *value*, so `Mass` and `Force` are genuinely distinct types and `mass + force` doesn't compile. `Length * Length → Area`, `Mass * Acceleration → Force`: the output type's dimension is `A * B`, computed at compile time by your dimension's own `const impl Mul`.
- **Units are constants.** A unit is a unit *of* `Q` through `impl Unit<Q>` - one per quantity (or point) it measures, in any crate that owns the quantity. Every quantity constant is one (`POUND(10.0)` builds, `KILOGRAM(m)` reads); a scale is a zero and a step (`CELSIUS`, `FEET_AMSL`, and on a logarithmic line `DBM`); anything else a type of yours with two functions.
- **Kinds: amounts, points, logarithms, and labels of yours.** A `#[kind]` field on `Dim` carries your own enum. Its ordinary variant is an amount; the dimension names which is affine (a point: an altitude, an instant) and which logarithmic (a `PowerLevel`, a `Gain`); any other variant is a label telling apart quantities that share their exponents - `yadc-si`'s `Absolute`, for `ThermodynamicTemperature` vs. `TemperatureInterval` - with its sums as data on the enum (`#[kind(sub(Absolute) = Linear)]`, `#[kind(add(Money) = Balance)]`, `= invalid`).
- **Points and levels behave as on the whiteboard.** altitude − altitude = length and altitude ± length = altitude, and nothing that depends on where the zero was put; level − level = gain and level ± gain = level, because adding logarithms multiplies.
- **Static and dynamic, mixed freely.** yadc is the statics; dynamic types are built with [`dynamickit`](dynamickit/), which knows nothing about quantities. `yadc-si`'s `DynamicSIQuantity` holds any dimension, carried at run time: every operator returns an `Option` (`None` for `kg + m`, or for a product of a level), so a calculation reads `((a * b)? + c)?`.
- **Units picked at run time.** A unit enum is a run-time choice within one family; `&dyn Unit<Length>` is "some unit of length", still checked at compile time; across quantities, your enum of unit enums is visited with `dynamickit`'s `Visit`, each family naming the quantity it measures in an associated type of yours.
- **Number types are yours too.** The stored number is whatever the quantity's field is: `f64`, `f32`, a newtype, a generic `N` (`Qty<const D: Dim, N = f64>(N)`, with `KILOPASCAL.cast::<f32>()` and a lossless std `From`). A unit counts in `f64` unless `#[unit(number = f32)]` says otherwise.
- **Nothing hidden.** The traits are the whole contract, and the derives write trait impls only; the quantity derive reaches dimensions only through `Dimension`; a derived unit delegates to the unit values you name in its attributes; only the crate that owns a quantity type writes its field.
- **Compile-time quantities.** Quantity arithmetic, constants and scales as units, and `cast` are `const`, so `const LIMIT: Velocity = 50.0 * KILOMETRE / HOUR;` is checked and evaluated by the compiler.
- **Optional serde** with a UCUM wire format for dimensions (`"m.kg.s-2"`, `"rad"`, `"K{Absolute}"`, `"1"`) via the `UcumSerialize` / `UcumDeserialize` derives, plus `UcumKind` for the label enum - all behind `yadc`'s `ucum` Cargo feature, which `yadc-si`'s `serde` feature turns on.
- **`no_std`.** `yadc`, `yadc-math` and `dynamickit` are `no_std`; a root or a level's logarithm reaches float maths through `num_traits`, so enable `yadc/libm` for a build without `std`.

## Examples

In [`yadc/examples/`](yadc/examples/):

- **`basic_use.rs`** — a tour of `yadc-si`: building and reading quantities with units (every constant is one), derived quantities, temperature scales (`CELSIUS`), power levels (`DBM`), `cast` and lossless `From`, printing, `DynamicSIQuantity`, units picked at run time, and `const` items built from the `consts` modules.
- **`define_unit_system.rs`** — a unit system from scratch, without `yadc-si`: `Kind`, `Dim`, `Qty`, base constants and everything defined from them (`PSI = POUND * STANDARD_GRAVITY / (INCH * INCH)`), and units of every shape - quantity constants, scales, levels in decibels and a type of yours (Beaufort), `f32` units, `&dyn` unit choices, units parsed from strings.
- **`generic_fns.rs`** — unit-system-agnostic functions bounded on `Q: Quantity`, `U: Unit<Q>` and the standard operators, called with SI and with a custom nautical system defined in the file.
- **`custom_unit_impl.rs`** — a unit written by hand (a run-time-calibrated sensor step): `impl Unit<Length>` counting in a `Length` field, plus `Display`, mixing with the derived SI units behind `&dyn Unit<Length>`.
- **`custom_value_type.rs`** — a custom number type with `const` operators, its constants, and units counting in it (`#[unit(number = ..)]`).
- **`kind_algebra.rs`** — a tour of the kinds: ordinary amounts, a point (a temperature), a logarithm (levels and gains), a label of the example's own, and the radian axis.
- **`fractional_exponents.rs`** — a dimension with fraction exponents (`yadc::Exponent`): an amplifier noise budget in V/√Hz, and CGS-Gaussian electrostatics.
- **`points.rs`** — points: the affine kind, zeros as constants (`SEA_LEVEL`, an epoch), the scales `FEET_AMSL` and `Scale(ground, FOOT)`, clearance and midpoints, and several zeros on one line.
- **`rotations.rs`** — rotations with real units: your own `Vec3<Q>`, a place in 3-D as a `Vec3` of points, and the site grid and a vehicle's frame as `Unit<Place, [f64; 3]>` values.
- **`serde_demo.rs`** — JSON round-trips of static and dynamic quantities, including the UCUM dimension string.
- **`uom.rs`** — `From` / `TryFrom` bridges between [`uom`](https://crates.io/crates/uom)'s SI quantities and `StaticSIQuantity` / `DynamicSIQuantity`.

In [`yadc-si/examples/`](yadc-si/examples/), using [`dynamickit`](dynamickit/): closed sets of quantities and their units, the dynamic type at run time, and readings that keep their unit (see each file's header).

In [`dynamickit/examples/`](dynamickit/examples/), nothing about units - fruit, sizes, colours and shapes, each example one feature with the hand-written equivalent of its derive beside it: `dynamic` (`Bag<F: Fruit>` → `AnyBag`: apples and oranges don't add), `dynamic_const` (lengths, areas and volumes: a const-parameter family whose fact has operators of its own), `members` (an RGB or an HSV colour: a plain enum, with `From` / `TryFrom` for its members), `lifting` (a method and a getter lifted), `visit` (one shape, and two at once), `members_via` (a cart computing through `AnyBag`), `pairing` (colours and the shapes they paint, related by an associated type), `variants` (a settings menu). One more, `dynamic_uom`, makes another crate's family dynamic: an `AnyQuantity` for [`uom`](https://crates.io/crates/uom)'s quantities, with run-time `*`, `/`, `+` and a checked way back.
